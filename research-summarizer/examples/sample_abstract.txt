Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide
range of natural language processing tasks, yet their tendency to produce factually
incorrect outputs -- commonly referred to as "hallucination" -- remains a significant
barrier to their deployment in high-stakes domains. In this work, we present a
systematic study of hallucination in LLMs, proposing a taxonomy that distinguishes
between intrinsic hallucinations (contradicting the source input) and extrinsic
hallucinations (unverifiable against the source). We introduce a benchmark of 5,000
human-annotated examples spanning summarization, question answering, and open-ended
generation, and evaluate eleven state-of-the-art models against it. Our results show
that model scale alone does not reduce hallucination rates, but that retrieval
augmentation and fine-tuning on human feedback each independently reduce extrinsic
hallucinations by over 30%. We further find that current automatic metrics correlate
poorly with human judgments of factuality, motivating the need for better evaluation
protocols. We release our benchmark and annotation guidelines to support future
research on mitigating hallucination in generative language models.
